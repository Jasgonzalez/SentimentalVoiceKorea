I have been living in Korea for six years now. When I first arrived, the only Korean words I knew were annyeonghaseyo and gamsahamnida. I’m from Kazakhstan, and my name is Madina. I came here when I was twenty-three, after growing up in the countryside and graduating from university in Astana, where I majored in leather production technology. My family still lives back home—my parents are in Kazakhstan, and my older sister lives in Germany. I am the youngest.

When I first visited Korea, it wasn’t supposed to be permanent. I came just to look around and find part-time work, but during that time I met the man who would become my husband. He’s Korean, and he doesn’t speak English, so from the very beginning we relied heavily on Google Translate—typing, voice translation, everything. He was shy and polite, and I was the one who suggested our first date. We didn’t understand each other’s language, but I felt a strong certainty about him, even before I fully understood my own feelings. We eventually married, and now I live in Korea as a marriage migrant. I’m twenty-eight now, and although we don’t have a baby yet, our life together continues to grow.

Life in Korea hasn’t always been easy. At first, my Korean was almost nonexistent, so working part-time jobs was a challenge. I started with dishwashing, then moved to more responsibilities, but restaurant jobs were difficult. Some customers and coworkers treated me poorly just because I was a foreigner. One moment I’ll never forget was when I accidentally put too many toppings on a salad. The team leader yelled at me: “If you don’t know Korean, just get out!” I didn’t even understand what I had done wrong at the time, but the words stayed with me. That moment made me determined to study Korean seriously—not just for work, but to protect my own feelings. I never wanted to feel helpless like that again.

But not every experience was negative. I also had part-time jobs where people were kind. I worked at a beef-soup restaurant where the manager often cooked food for me, and we would chat during quiet hours. I enjoyed talking to customers when I worked as a server. Those memories balanced the difficult ones and helped me feel more connected to life here.

I began studying Korean whenever I could—sometimes in classes, sometimes just alone at home. I eventually passed level 4, but when I tried to continue to level 5, I had to stop because my father suddenly collapsed back home. After returning to Korea, I kept switching between working and studying because money was tight. Eventually, I decided to prepare for a nail-care certification. The written exam was incredibly difficult, filled with vocabulary I had never learned before. I failed fourteen times in one year. But I didn’t give up. On my fifteenth attempt, I finally passed. Not only that—I passed the practical exam on my first try. Earning this certificate was a huge accomplishment for me, and although I haven't started my own business yet, I hope to gain experience working in a salon first.

Recently, I went to a few job interviews, but some places told me they needed someone with experience, and others never responded. One salon was too far from where I live. For now, I’ve decided to prioritize studying Korean again, attending classes three days a week—from Tuesday to Thursday. On the other days, I keep looking for part-time work. I also love art, music, and reading. Sometimes I draw in sketchbooks. I still want to try new things, and soon I’ll be starting work at a Nike store. I hope it will be a good new experience.

Even with the challenges, I’ve grown used to life here. Kazakhstan’s economy is still developing, and Korea feels more advanced and stable. I’ve adapted to so many things I never imagined when I was younger. Right now, I don’t want to return home. Maybe my feelings will change when I grow older, but for now, I imagine myself living in a quiet rural part of Korea, surrounded by mountains and nature. A peaceful life in the countryside sounds beautiful to me. If I could live in a village like that someday, I think I would be happy.
